Attribute Checks and Skill Checks

Most ordin­ary activ­ity needs no roll. Make a check when the result is genu­inely uncer­tain and success or failure matters.

An Attrib­ute check resolves some­thing a plaus­ible char­ac­ter could attempt without a named body of train­ing. Roll d20 equal to or below the rel­ev­ant raw Attrib­ute score after apply­ing dif­fi­culty and other mod­i­fi­ers.

A Skill check resolves a task gov­erned by a named Skill. A trained char­ac­ter begins with the full rel­ev­ant Attrib­ute score. A char­ac­ter who lacks the Skill follows its untrained rule; the same task does not become an Attrib­ute check merely because the untrained threshold is less favor­able.

A short, ordin­ary jump may call for a Dex­ter­ity check. A dif­fi­cult leap that requires trained body control may call for Acro­bat­ics instead.

Choosing the Governing Attribute

Each Skill lists its usual Attrib­ute, which is the normal start­ing point for its checks. The DM may choose another Attrib­ute when the char­ac­ter genu­inely uses a dif­fer­ent method.

Intim­id­a­tion ordin­ar­ily uses Cha­risma when the char­ac­ter relies on pres­ence, threats, or force of per­son­al­ity. A display of phys­ical menace may use Strength instead. The Skill remains Intim­id­a­tion; only the Attrib­ute gov­ern­ing that attempt changes.

Trained and Untrained Use

A trained Skill check begins with the full gov­ern­ing Attrib­ute. An untrained attempt that the Skill permits begins with half the gov­ern­ing Attrib­ute, rounded down. Apply dif­fi­culty and other mod­i­fi­ers after estab­lish­ing that start­ing threshold.

The Untrained line in each Skill entry uses one of three labels:

Untrained Meaning
Yes Ordinary uses may be attempted untrained.
Limited Basic or obvious uses may be attempted untrained. Technical, professional, or hazardous uses require training.
Trained only The named Skill use requires training. An ordinary nontechnical action may still be an Attribute check when it does not require that body of knowledge.

For example, a char­ac­ter with Dex­ter­ity 15 begins a trained Acro­bat­ics check at 15. If the attemp­ted use permits an untrained check, the char­ac­ter instead begins at 7 after round­ing down. Normal dif­fi­culty lowers that untrained threshold to 5.

Difficulty

After estab­lish­ing the check's start­ing threshold, apply the task's dif­fi­culty:

Difficulty Easy Normal Hard Very Hard Extreme
Modifier — -2 -4 -6 -8

Easy rep­res­ents a mean­ing­ful check under favor­able or ordin­ary cir­cum­stances. Normal intro­duces some risk or com­plic­a­tion. Hard, Very Hard, and Extreme rep­res­ent increas­ingly demand­ing cir­cum­stances, with Extreme forming the prac­tical end of the ordin­ary ladder. A spe­cific Skill, Talent, spell, or other rule may state another mod­i­fier when its own pro­ced­ure requires one.

Bonuses raise the threshold; pen­al­ties and dif­fi­culty lower it. Roll d20. A result equal to or below the final threshold suc­ceeds. Natural 1 and natural 20 have no sep­ar­ate auto­matic effect on a roll-under check; compare the result with the threshold nor­mally. An unmod­i­fied threshold of 20 cannot fail an ordin­ary d20 check.

Opposed Checks and Check Margin

When two char­ac­ters dir­ectly oppose one another, both make the required check. For each par­ti­cipant, sub­tract the d20 result from the adjus­ted threshold. The result is the char­ac­ter's check margin.

The higher check margin wins, even when both margins are neg­at­ive. A tie favors the defender. Check margin is sep­ar­ate from an attack's Margin of Success.

When a rule says that an opposed check suc­ceeds by 8 or more, the winning check margin must exceed the losing margin by at least 8. In an unop­posed check, failure by 8 or more means a check margin of -8 or lower.

Example: Opposed Check Margins

One par­ti­cipant has an adjus­ted threshold of 20 and rolls 23, pro­du­cing a check margin of -3. The other has a threshold of 18 and rolls 20, pro­du­cing a check margin of -2. Both rolled above their thresholds, but -2 is the higher margin, so the second par­ti­cipant wins. If both margins were equal, the defender would win.

Time, Attempts, and Repeated Effort

A check takes however long the attemp­ted task nat­ur­ally requires. A spe­cific Skill use, Talent, combat option, or other rule states an exact action or dur­a­tion when timing matters.

One roll ordin­ar­ily rep­res­ents the com­plete attempt. Failure does not grant unlim­ited con­sequence-free rerolls through the same method. Another attempt becomes pos­sible when the task nat­ur­ally permits repeated effort, but each attempt con­sumes its normal time and carries the cir­cum­stances and con­sequences of con­tinu­ing.

A char­ac­ter may keep trying to force a door, for example, while every attempt takes time and makes noise. Research­ing the same exhausted source again does not produce another answer unless the char­ac­ter brings new inform­a­tion, gains access to dif­fer­ent mater­ial, or changes the method.

Skills and Talents

Skills describe learned, repeat­able com­pet­en­cies. Talents grant excep­tional train­ing or abil­it­ies that may use, improve, replace, or auto­mat­ic­ally resolve a Skill check.

Stealth remains the ordin­ary Skill behind con­ceal­ment and quiet move­ment; Hide and Sneak provide their own excep­tional bene­fits. Sur­vival sup­ports delib­er­ate exam­in­a­tion of tracks; the Track­ing Talent permits much faster recog­ni­tion and pursuit of usable signs. Medi­cine covers dia­gnosis and con­tinu­ing care; Field Medic provides urgent treat­ment under field pres­sure.

When a Talent changes a Skill, use the Talent's com­plete rule. Learn­ing the Skill alone does not grant the Talent, and learn­ing the Talent does not trans­form it into a second Skill.

Learning Skills and Skill Mastery

Starting Skills

A new char­ac­ter receives start­ing Skills in three parts:

  1. Choose three Skills appro­pri­ate to the train­ing of the char­ac­ter's start­ing Job. Choose from the full Skill cata­logue, subject to DM approval.
  2. Add unres­tric­ted Skill choices equal to the char­ac­ter's pos­it­ive per­man­ent Intel­li­gence mod­i­fier after racial and prime-attrib­ute adjust­ments. A mod­i­fier of +0 or lower adds no choices and never removes any of the three Job-based choices.
  3. A Human adds one further unres­tric­ted Skill choice.

A repeated choice must be replaced rather than learned twice. Each sep­ar­ately named Craft, Know­ledge, Per­form­ance, or Pro­fes­sion field is a sep­ar­ate Skill. Know­ledge (History) and Know­ledge (Lan­guage: Ayamaoan), for example, are two dif­fer­ent choices.

Lan­guages granted by region, race, or a start­ing Mage Job cost no start­ing Skill choices; Char­ac­ter Cre­ation lists those grants. Every addi­tional lan­guage is its own Know­ledge (Lan­guage: spe­cific lan­guage) Skill and uses one start­ing Skill choice unless another rule grants it. Learn­ing a special lan­guage during cre­ation requires DM per­mis­sion and appro­pri­ate access.

Start­ing Skill choices grant trained Skills, not Skill Mas­ter­ies. Intel­li­gence determ­ines how many Skill Mas­ter­ies the char­ac­ter may even­tu­ally know; it does not grant those Mas­ter­ies auto­mat­ic­ally. Acquir­ing another Job later does not repeat these cre­ation-only choices.

Learning a New Skill

Learn­ing a new Skill after char­ac­ter cre­ation nor­mally costs 10 XP. The DM may raise the cost as high as 30 XP accord­ing to how dif­fi­cult or unfa­mil­iar that train­ing is for the char­ac­ter and the cir­cum­stances in which it is being learned.

The char­ac­ter's exist­ing Jobs, back­ground, exper­i­ence, access to inform­a­tion, and oppor­tun­ity to prac­tice all matter. A Sor­cerer accus­tomed to lib­rar­ies and magical study may find Sur­vival unusu­ally dif­fi­cult, while a career Warrior may have the same dif­fi­culty learn­ing Know­ledge (Magic). This is a judg­ment made for the char­ac­ter and situ­ation, not a fixed com­pat­ib­il­ity table.

A suit­able instructor may halve the final XP cost, to a minimum of 5 XP.

Skill Mastery

A Skill Mastery unlocks the advanced uses described in that Skill's Mastery inset. It grants no uni­ver­sal numer­ical bonus and never guar­an­tees success. When the outcome remains uncer­tain, roll the under­ly­ing Skill nor­mally.

A char­ac­ter must know a Skill before learn­ing its Mastery, and the Mastery must be learned or pur­chased; it is never gained auto­mat­ic­ally. Each Skill's Mastery can be learned only once and never stacks with itself.

The number of Skill Mas­ter­ies a char­ac­ter may know cannot exceed the char­ac­ter's per­man­ent Intel­li­gence mod­i­fier, with a minimum capa­city of one. Tem­por­ary effects and equip­ment do not create addi­tional capa­city.

Learn­ing a Skill Mastery takes at least six months and ordin­ar­ily takes about one year. It requires proper tutel­age: a qual­i­fied teacher, a uni­ver­sity or com­par­able insti­tu­tion, or another source capable of provid­ing sus­tained advanced train­ing.

A Skill Mastery costs between 10 and 30 XP, as determ­ined by the DM accord­ing to the field, the avail­able tutel­age, and the cir­cum­stances of the train­ing.

Reading a Skill Entry

Each entry begins with the Skill's usual Attrib­ute and its Untrained label. The usual Attrib­ute governs most attempts, although a genu­inely dif­fer­ent method may call for another Attrib­ute. The Untrained label states whether a char­ac­ter without the Skill may attempt all ordin­ary uses, only basic uses, or none of the named Skill uses.

The descrip­tion iden­ti­fies the tasks the Skill com­monly governs. A Related Talent line appears when a con­firmed Talent builds on that Skill and helps the reader find the excep­tional pro­ced­ure. A Skill Mastery section appears only when that Skill has a com­plete advanced-use rule.

Acrobatics

Usual Attribute: Dexterity Untrained: Yes

Acro­bat­ics covers demand­ing move­ment that depends on balance, coordin­a­tion, and precise control. A char­ac­ter might use it to cross a narrow beam or rain-slick roof, keep footing on a swaying bridge, nego­ti­ate loose rubble, land under control after a dif­fi­cult drop, or place the body pre­cisely during a dif­fi­cult leap.

Use Ath­let­ics when height, dis­tance, force, or sus­tained exer­tion is the central chal­lenge. Acro­bat­ics applies when balance, place­ment, or a con­trolled landing matters most.

Related Talent: Tum­bling. Tum­bling builds on Acro­bat­ics for excep­tional move­ment.

Animal Handling

Usual Attribute: Wisdom Untrained: Yes

Animal Hand­ling governs imme­di­ate inter­ac­tion with an animal and the ability to read its present beha­vior. It may help a char­ac­ter approach a nervous horse, settle a pack animal frightened by noise or blood, lead live­stock through a crowded place, restrain an agit­ated dog, or recog­nize signs of fear, pain, aggres­sion, or exhaus­tion.

The animal's tem­pera­ment, famili­ar­ity with the char­ac­ter, treat­ment, sur­round­ings, and current dis­tress shape the attempt. Teach­ing lasting com­mands and prac­ticed beha­vior uses Animal Train­ing. When someone is already in the saddle, Ride governs the rider's control while Animal Hand­ling remains rel­ev­ant when the animal's dis­pos­i­tion is the real dif­fi­culty.

Animal Training

Usual Attribute: Wisdom Untrained: Limited

Animal Train­ing teaches lasting com­mands, estab­lishes routines, and accus­toms an animal to unfa­mil­iar demands. It can teach an animal to halt, return, carry a rider or load, accept harness, remain man­age­able around crowds or loud activ­ity, and perform work appro­pri­ate to its nature and train­ing.

Train­ing takes the time appro­pri­ate to the animal and the beha­vior being taught. Tem­pera­ment, prior treat­ment, danger, and the com­plex­ity of the desired beha­vior shape the task.

Animal Training · continued

An untrained char­ac­ter may rein­force a simple beha­vior in a calm, famil­iar animal. Dan­ger­ous animals and demand­ing or pro­fes­sional work require the Skill. Imme­di­ate attempts to calm, lead, or restrain an animal use Animal Hand­ling.

Skill Mastery: Animal Training

Animal Train­ing Skill Mastery unlocks complex train­ing. A char­ac­ter with this Mastery can teach an animal linked routines made up of several famil­iar actions, rehab­il­it­ate entrenched fear or hos­til­ity caused by prior treat­ment, and train unusual animals that remain nat­ur­ally capable of learn­ing the desired beha­vior.

The animal's nature and intel­li­gence still limit what it can learn, and train­ing still requires suit­able treat­ment, con­di­tions, and time.

This Mastery does not charm an animal, create super­nat­ural loyalty or an animal com­pan­ion, or grant the Tame Talent. It does not make an animal Combat Trained; the normal require­ments for combat train­ing still apply.

Appraisal

Usual Attribute: Intelligence Untrained: Limited

Appraisal estim­ates an object's quality, con­di­tion, work­man­ship, authen­ti­city, and likely value. It can dis­tin­guish careful crafts­man­ship from dec­or­at­ive show, recog­nize repairs or sub­sti­tu­tions, compare two pieces of appar­ently similar quality, and judge whether an object matches its claimed mater­ial or origin.

Famil­iar goods and obvious defects may be judged untrained. Train­ing becomes import­ant when value depends on rare mater­i­als, subtle work­man­ship, con­cealed repairs, spe­cial­ist con­struc­tion, or a con­vin­cing imit­a­tion.

Appraisal exam­ines the object itself. Com­merce con­cerns the market, pro­spect­ive buyers, and terms of sale. A rel­ev­ant Craft may reveal tech­nical flaws that a general appraisal cannot estab­lish.

Athletics

Usual Attribute: Strength Untrained: Yes

Ath­let­ics covers force­ful or sus­tained phys­ical move­ment. Its ordin­ary uses include climb­ing a rough wall or rope, pulling oneself over a ledge, swim­ming through dif­fi­cult water, sprint­ing across demand­ing ground, clear­ing a ditch, or jumping high enough to seize a hand­hold.

Bluff

Usual Attribute: Charisma Untrained: Yes

Bluff lets a char­ac­ter present false­hood, mis­dir­ec­tion, or con­cealed intent as some­thing plaus­ible. It covers spoken lies, feigned con­fid­ence, mis­lead­ing beha­vior, inven­ted explan­a­tions, and dis­trac­tions created through words or conduct.

A decep­tion becomes easier to believe when it fits what the listener already expects and agrees with the evid­ence at hand. Firsthand know­ledge, obvious con­tra­dic­tions, close scru­tiny, or a story that demands too much faith make the attempt harder.

At a city gate, a trav­eler might claim that a delayed wagon is expec­ted inside, answer ques­tions with rehearsed con­fid­ence, and keep the guard's atten­tion away from a com­pan­ion. Pre­par­ing a false pass would call for Forgery, while taking the guard's key would call for Sleight of Hand.

Cartography

Usual Attribute: Intelligence Untrained: Limited

Car­to­graphy covers reading, copying, cor­rect­ing, and cre­at­ing maps. A car­to­grapher can inter­pret symbols and scale, compare con­flict­ing maps, trans­late meas­ure­ments and route notes into a usable rep­res­ent­a­tion, update a map after the land­scape has changed, and recog­nize when dis­tances, land­marks, or terrain do not fit together.

Anyone may sketch a famil­iar road or mark a remembered land­mark. Pro­du­cing an accur­ate working map, repair­ing an incom­plete chart, or com­bin­ing several imper­fect accounts requires train­ing.

Car­to­graphy records what is known about the land. Nav­ig­a­tion uses that inform­a­tion, together with obser­va­tion and remembered geo­graphy, to determ­ine pos­i­tion and course.

Command

Usual Attribute: Charisma Untrained: Yes

Command gives clear orders, assigns imme­di­ate respons­ib­il­it­ies, and keeps people working together when speed, danger, noise, or con­fu­sion could break their coordin­a­tion. It applies to a crew, patrol, work gang, nearby mil­it­ary unit, or similar group the char­ac­ter can per­son­ally direct.

A com­mander might assign a patrol to several entrances, organ­ize workers around a col­lapsing struc­ture, call a deck crew to prepare for a squall, or restore order after con­flict­ing instruc­tions. The Skill helps make pri­or­it­ies under­stood and keeps atten­tion on the shared task.

Command is per­sonal and imme­di­ate: the char­ac­ter must be able to com­mu­nic­ate with the group and observe the work closely enough to direct it. Use Mass Combat when dir­ect­ing form­a­tions in battle and the Ruler System when gov­ern­ing estates, armies, courts, fac­tions, or coun­tries.

Commerce

Usual Attribute: Intelligence Untrained: Yes

Com­merce is prac­tical know­ledge of markets, trade customs, con­tracts, supply, bar­gain­ing, and the move­ment of goods. It helps a char­ac­ter locate buyers or sellers, recog­nize scarcity or unusual prices, under­stand the terms of a trans­ac­tion, and judge how mer­chand­ise reaches a region.

A mer­chant might identify the market most likely to accept foreign coin, dis­cover which caravan con­trols the local salt supply, nego­ti­ate a bulk pur­chase, or notice that a con­tract quietly trans­fers an unusual risk to the buyer. Avail­able altern­at­ives, urgency, repu­ta­tion, and know­ledge of the market all shape the bargain.

Appraisal can estab­lish the work­man­ship and likely value of a jeweled cup. Com­merce finds the buyer who wants it, pre­dicts what that buyer may pay, and nego­ti­ates the terms of sale.

Craft governs the prac­tical work of a learned trade, from select­ing mater­i­als and plan­ning the task through shaping, fitting, assem­bling, fin­ish­ing, and repair. Each field is a sep­ar­ate Skill.

Cryptography

Usual Attribute: Intelligence Untrained: Trained only

Cryp­to­graphy covers recog­niz­ing, cre­at­ing, and deci­pher­ing delib­er­ate codes and ciphers. It exam­ines repeated symbols, sub­sti­tu­tions, pat­terns, keys, con­ven­tional open­ings, and the struc­ture of a message to under­stand how its con­tents have been con­cealed.

A cryp­to­grapher might compare several inter­cep­ted mes­sages for a shared system, prepare a message using an estab­lished code, recog­nize that appar­ently ordin­ary writing con­tains an encoded pattern, or work from context and sur­viv­ing frag­ments to recon­struct a cipher.

Deci­pher­ing the code reveals the message as written; it does not grant fluency in its lan­guage. Unfa­mil­iar lan­guages and scripts use Lin­guist­ics or the rel­ev­ant Know­ledge field.

Diplomacy

Dip­lomacy per­suades, nego­ti­ates, medi­ates dis­putes, and presents terms in a form another person may accept. It can secure a hearing, request aid or access, improve strained rela­tions, broker an exchange, calm a dispute, or find a work­able com­prom­ise between com­pet­ing interests.

Engineering

Usual Attribute: Intelligence Untrained: Trained only

Engin­eer­ing covers the design and ana­lysis of struc­tures, machines, for­ti­fic­a­tions, bridges, water­works, and com­par­able con­struc­ted systems. It con­siders sup­ports, loads, stresses, moving parts, mater­i­als, drain­age, and likely points of failure.

A sagging bridge can be examined for failing sup­ports, a hoist judged against its inten­ded load, and a for­ti­fic­a­tion studied for vul­ner­able points. Engin­eer­ing also plans the passage of water through a mill or set­tle­ment and tests whether a pro­posed alter­a­tion will weaken a larger struc­ture.

Engin­eer­ing sup­plies the design and tech­nical judg­ment. The rel­ev­ant Craft carries out con­struc­tion, fitting, and repair.

Skill Mastery: Engineering

Escape Artist

Usual Attribute: Dexterity Untrained: Yes

Escape Artist uses flex­ib­il­ity, lever­age, and careful move­ment to slip bonds, work free of a net or entangling wrap, squeeze through a con­fined opening, or release a trapped limb. A char­ac­ter might search for slack in tied wrists, turn the body through a gap barely large enough to permit passage, or free a boot caught between stones.

Etiquette

Usual Attribute: Charisma Untrained: Yes

Etiquette is fluency in the manners, titles, cere­mon­ies, and expect­a­tions of struc­tured society. It helps a char­ac­ter choose the proper form of address, make intro­duc­tions, exchange gifts, observe pre­ced­ence, dress for an occa­sion, and move through formal gath­er­ings without cre­at­ing an unin­ten­ded insult.

The Skill can also reveal meaning carried through cere­mony. A change in seating may signal favor or dis­grace; an omitted title may be a care­less mistake or a delib­er­ate slight; an unusu­ally valu­able gift may create an expect­a­tion that nobody states aloud.

Sleight of Hand

Usual Attribute: Dexterity Untrained: Yes

Sleight of Hand uses quick, con­trolled move­ment to palm, conceal, pass, switch, or remove a small object without attract­ing notice. It can slip a coin into a sleeve, exchange one small object for another, pass some­thing between com­pan­ions, hide an item during a search, manip­u­late a game piece, or take advant­age of a brief dis­trac­tion.

The object's size and pos­i­tion, the avail­able con­ceal­ment, the observer's atten­tion, and the char­ac­ter's oppor­tun­ity shape the dif­fi­culty. Manip­u­lat­ing an unat­ten­ded object beneath a crowded table is easier than remov­ing some­thing worn by an alert person in plain view.

Stealth

Usual Attribute: Dexterity Untrained: Yes

Stealth governs ordin­ary attempts to avoid notice through quiet move­ment, careful pos­i­tion­ing, con­trolled noise, and the use of con­ceal­ment. A char­ac­ter may choose a route that stays behind cover, wait for a guard's atten­tion to turn, cross a noisy surface care­fully, keep equip­ment from strik­ing together, or coordin­ate a group's move­ment through dark­ness or dis­trac­tion.

A Stealth attempt begins from cir­cum­stances that provide a plaus­ible route or place to remain unnoticed, such as cover, dark­ness, dis­trac­tion, or broken lines of sight. Changes in those cir­cum­stances may create a new oppor­tun­ity, expose the char­ac­ter, or alter what con­tin­ued con­ceal­ment requires.

The observer's atten­tion and the char­ac­ter's method determ­ine whether the check is unop­posed or opposed under the ordin­ary check rules. Moving across noisy ground, passing close to an alert guard, or con­ceal­ing more than one person may change the dif­fi­culty.

Related Talents: Hide and Sneak. Both build on Stealth for excep­tional con­ceal­ment and quiet move­ment.

Survival

Usual Attribute: Wisdom Untrained: Yes

Sur­vival covers living and trav­el­ling safely beyond settled places. It may be used to find food and water, make a defens­ible or weather­proof camp, protect sup­plies from the ele­ments, judge approach­ing weather, identify natural hazards, and choose a prac­tical line of travel through dif­fi­cult country.

Sur­vival also sup­ports delib­er­ate exam­in­a­tion of phys­ical tracks when usable signs remain. Foot­prints, dis­turbed soil, broken veget­a­tion, dung, ashes, and dis­carded mater­ial may reveal a dir­ec­tion of travel and other con­clu­sions jus­ti­fied by the sur­viv­ing evid­ence. Poor ground, weather, age, con­ceal­ment, and inter­rup­ted signs can make the task more dif­fi­cult or limit what can reas­on­ably be learned.

Nav­ig­a­tion determ­ines pos­i­tion and course. Sur­vival keeps people sup­plied and safe while cross­ing the terrain.

Related Talent: Track­ing. Track­ing builds on Sur­vival for rapid recog­ni­tion and pursuit of usable signs.

Skill Mastery: Survival

Sur­vival Skill Mastery unlocks trail recon­struc­tion. A char­ac­ter with this Mastery can recon­struct the likely route between sep­ar­ated sur­viv­ing signs and reac­quire a trail after its con­tinu­ous trace has been lost, con­cealed, or delib­er­ately broken, provided enough phys­ical evid­ence remains.

Trail recon­struc­tion takes the time appro­pri­ate to careful exam­in­a­tion. It can bridge gaps jus­ti­fied by sur­viv­ing signs, but it cannot create evid­ence where no usable signs remain.

Track­ing retains its extraordin­ary speed of recog­ni­tion and pursuit. Trail­craft remains a sep­ar­ate Scout feature. It grants its listed +4 to par­tic­u­lar Sur­vival checks for food, water, shelter, weather, and natural travel hazards; the bonus does not extend to trail recon­struc­tion, fol­low­ing tracks, Nav­ig­a­tion, or avoid­ing becom­ing lost.

Additional Skills and Fields

The cata­logue presents common recur­ring Skills rather than every field that can exist. When a new dis­cip­line becomes import­ant during play, the DM may intro­duce an appro­pri­ate Skill or Know­ledge field; a char­ac­ter is not pen­al­ized for lacking a Skill that had not pre­vi­ously existed as an avail­able choice.
