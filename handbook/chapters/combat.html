Values Used in Combat

This section is a map of the combat system. The later sections supply the complete procedures and exceptions.

Ability modifier
The modifier derived from an ability score. Combat formulas use the modifier unless a rule explicitly calls for the full score or a roll-under ability check.
Main Job and Job level
Each Job keeps its own level. The highest-level Job is the Main Job; if several are tied, the one acquired first remains Main. An ability tied to a particular Job uses the level of the Job that granted it.
Base Attack Bonus (BAB)
The character's core combat bonus. BAB contributes to Attack, Dodge, Parry, Block, and many combat maneuvers.
Initiative
1d20 + Dexterity modifier + explicit initiative modifiers. Initiative establishes the order in which combatants act.
Round and initiative pass
A round contains one or more sweeps through initiative order. The first sweep is the first initiative pass; later passes resolve additional attacks. Spells complete at their scheduled Initiative values.
Movement and Main Action
Separate allowances normally used on the first pass. A Main Action may be a single attack, Full Attack, spell, or another legal action.
Primary Attack
Your character's primary method of attacking. Choose it when declaring a Full Attack; it supplies that Full Attack's ordinary attacks. Unless stated otherwise, Attack Speed increases apply to the Primary Attack.
Attack Speed
The number of attack passes supplied by the Primary Attack during a Full Attack. Fractional speeds alternate between a higher and lower round, beginning every combat with the higher round.
Single attack and Full Attack
A single attack uses the Main Action for one attack. When declaring a Full Attack, choose its Primary Attack and optional abilities; the resulting attacks form its schedule. The Full Attack spends 1 PE and ordinarily resolves no more than five attacks.
Attack and Defense
The attacker rolls against the defender's declared Dodge, Parry, or Block. The defender may instead use Passive Defense: 10 + BAB + applicable passive magical defensive modifiers. The choice is made before either combat roll. An Attack that meets or exceeds Defense connects.
Margin of Success
Attack - Defense. A tied result hits with Margin 0. A positive Margin is added to damage.
Armor Penetration (Pen) and Armor Grade (Grade)
Pen measures an attack's ability to defeat physical armor. Grade measures how difficult that armor is to defeat. For armor with a Grade, compare them to determine how much of its listed DR applies.
Damage Reduction (DR)
Protection subtracted from damage before Physical Energy mitigation and before damage reaches Hit Points or Life Points.
Hit Points (HP) and Life Points (LP)
HP are one protective pool increased by Jobs, training, worn armor, and other effects. Temporary HP is separate only when its source says so. LP represent bodily survival. Ordinary damage depletes HP before reaching LP.
Physical Energy (PE) and Magical Energy (ME)
PE pays for strenuous physical options, including a Full Attack. ME pays spell costs. Neither pool may be spent for a purpose unless a rule permits it.

ART
A trained value added to every Difficulty Class the character creates.
Saving throw and Difficulty Class (DC)
A save is 1d20 + base save + governing ability modifier + explicit bonuses against the effect's DC. Meeting the DC succeeds; the effect states what success accomplishes.
Roll-under and opposed checks
Roll 1d20 equal to or below the adjusted threshold. Check margin is threshold - roll. When a combat procedure opposes two such checks, compare check margins and favor the defender on a tie.
Advantage, Disadvantage, and rerolls
Advantage rolls 2d20 and keeps the higher die; Disadvantage keeps the lower. Sources cancel one for one, and any remainder applies only once. A reroll normally replaces one die; a Luck reroll keeps the more favorable original or revised result.
Spell Speed
The delay between beginning a spell and its completion. Spell completion is scheduled within the initiative countdown independently of Attack Speed.
Surprised and Flat-Footed
A Surprised creature is genuinely unaware of an attack or danger. A Flat-Footed creature has become aware too late for a complete defensive response. Both use restricted static Defenses described under Beginning Combat.
Domain Rank
The D/C/B/A/AA/S scale of the value governing an offense, defense, check, or other opposed effect. Rank gaps add their own modifier without changing the underlying statistic or its ordinary modifier; the complete procedure appears under Power Scale and Domain Ranks.

The Combat Sequence

Use this sequence for an ordinary encounter:

  1. Begin combat. A hostile action starts mechanical combat. The DM determines who is involved, who perceives the danger, and who is Surprised or Flat-Footed.
  2. Roll initiative. Every current participant rolls Initiative. A character drawn into combat during a round rolls to enter on the next round.
  3. Resolve Round 0 when needed. Eligible aware creatures receive either Movement or one Main Action that is not a Full Attack. If everyone expected the fight, begin with Round 1 instead.
  4. Establish current attack modifiers. Apply any Attack-Speed changes that begin this round. When a character declares a Full Attack, choose its Primary Attack and optional additions, then establish that Full Attack's exact schedule.
  5. Resolve the first initiative pass. Act from highest Initiative to lowest. Each combatant may use Movement and one Main Action, subject to conditions and other restrictions. A Full Attack costs 1 PE and begins its sequence of attacks. A maneuver may replace the Main Action or an attack only when its own procedure permits it.
  6. Resolve each attack. Declare the target and attack. The defender declares a legal active Defense or Passive Defense. Roll Attack and any active Defense, calculate Margin of Success on a hit, and calculate raw damage. Compare Pen with Grade when the armor has a Grade, apply the resulting DR and any flat DR, apply eligible PE mitigation, then route the remainder through HP and LP.
  7. Resolve later passes. Return to the top of Initiative for combatants with unresolved Full-Attack passes. Later passes normally grant no Movement or unrestricted Main Action. Resolve scheduled spell completions at their proper Initiative values.
  8. End the round. After every attack pass and scheduled completion has resolved, apply anything timed for the end of a turn or round. Begin the next round and advance any fractional Attack Speed schedule.

Example: One Complete Ordinary Combat Round

A Mercenary and a guard expect a fight, so there is no Round 0. The Mercenary has Initiative 17, Attack Speed 1.5, Strength modifier +3, and a Medium 1d8 sword with Pen 2. The guard has Initiative 12, Attack Speed 1, a sword and shield, Medium armor with Grade 2, DR 2, and Armor HP 6, and 8 HP before armor. The worn armor therefore raises the guard's maximum HP to 14.

First pass, Initiative 17. The Mercenary moves 20 feet into reach, declares a Full Attack, and spends 1 PE. Because this is the first round of combat, Attack Speed 1.5 provides two attack passes. The guard declares Dodge before the dice are rolled.

The Mercenary's Attack total is 24 and the guard's Dodge is 20. The attack connects with Margin 4. The sword rolls 5 damage, producing 5 + 3 Strength + 4 Margin = 12 raw damage. Pen 2 equals Grade 2, so the armor's normal DR 2 reduces this to 10. The damage reduces the guard's 14 HP to 4 HP. The Armor HP is part of that same HP total rather than a separately tracked damage pool.

First pass, Initiative 12. The guard uses the Main Action for one attack. The Mercenary declares Parry. The guard's Attack is 18 and the Mercenary's Parry is 21, so the attack misses.

Second pass, Initiative 17. Only the Mercenary has another attack pass. No ordinary Movement is available. The guard declares Block. Attack and Block both total 20, so the attack connects with Margin 0. The sword rolls 4, producing 4 + 3 Strength + 0 Margin = 7 raw damage. DR 2 reduces this to 5. The guard's remaining 4 HP are removed and the final 1 damage reaches LP.

No other combatant has an attack pass or scheduled completion, so the round ends. In Round 2, the lower side of the Mercenary's Attack Speed 1.5 schedule provides one attack pass.

Beginning Combat

Combat begins when a hostile situation enters structured rounds. Awareness determines who can act during the opening moments and what defenses are available before everyone can respond normally.

When Combat Begins and Who Participates

Initiative begins when someone declares or begins an action that starts mechanical combat. Tension, threats, visible weapons, nearby danger, or the DM's private knowledge of an enemy do not place the table into combat by themselves.

The initiating action does not need to inflict damage. During a hostile confrontation, casting an overt spell, activating a protective effect, making a threatening movement, or taking a similar action may be enough. The DM decides whether the action begins combat from the circumstances.

Use the following order:

  1. Declare the initiating action.
  1. Determine each creature's awareness and whether anyone is Surprised.
  1. Identify the creatures currently involved.
  1. Roll initiative before resolving the initiating action.
  1. Resolve Surprise Round 0 if one is required; otherwise begin Round 1.

Every creature the DM considers part of the initial hostile exchange rolls initiative, including a creature that is Surprised. When everyone involved understands that violence is beginning, skip Round 0 and begin Round 1. Combatants who have exchanged threats, approached with weapons, formed battle lines, or otherwise expected the fight defend normally from the opening attack. Losing initiative in an expected confrontation does not by itself make a creature Flat-Footed.

An attack prepared before initiative is not a Readied Action and does not grant a free attack before combat. Everyone currently involved still rolls initiative. Credible preparation may receive a situational initiative bonus or lead the DM to resolve the opening through Round 0. When the prepared attack occurs, it uses the attacker's normal Main Action.

Awareness and Surprise

The DM determines awareness separately for each creature. Perception, concealment, deception, Stealth, speed, preparation, and the surrounding circumstances may call for checks, but there is no universal Surprise roll.

Use the following awareness ladder:

  • A creature that cannot perceive the hostile creature, attack, or danger at all is Surprised.
  • A creature that knows an unseen attacker is present but cannot locate or anticipate that attacker is Flat-Footed against that attacker.
  • A creature that can perceive, locate, or otherwise anticipate an attacker is neither Surprised nor Flat-Footed merely because it cannot see the attacker.

Invisibility and Blindness do not determine awareness automatically. Sound, movement, behavior, special senses, Blind Fighting, and the circumstances determine what a creature can perceive or anticipate.

Surprise Round 0

If at least one current participant is aware of the opposition while another is genuinely unaware, resolve a special Surprise Round 0 before Round 1.

Round 0 is a brief reaction phase, commonly representing only two or three seconds. Only creatures aware enough to participate may act, in initiative order. Each eligible creature receives either:

  • its Movement; or
  • one Main Action that is not a Full Attack.

The creature never receives both. It may make one weapon attack, begin casting a spell, give a meaningful warning or order, or perform another legal single action. Round 0 never grants a Full Attack, additional attack passes, or an additional action from Haste.

Brief speech directly accompanying an action may be free. A warning, order, or other communication that gives someone usable information consumes the Round 0 action.

Drawing or readying an ordinary accessible weapon uses the creature's Movement. The creature can draw and attack during Round 0 only when an explicit Quick Draw permission applies to that weapon.

A spell begun during Round 0 spends its full Magical Energy (ME) and follows its normal Spell Speed. Only eligible Round 0 participants take actions while the countdown advances. A numeric Spell Speed can continue below initiative 0 and still complete during Round 0; a casting time measured in full rounds or longer units may continue into Round 1 or later according to the spell.

The once-per-battle abilities granted by the Warrior and Adventurer Chassis are unavailable during Round 0. They become available when Round 1 begins.

Round 0 is separate from Round 1. It does not consume the front-loaded round of a fractional Attack Speed schedule. When Round 1 begins, per-round Dodge, Parry, Block, and attack-of-opportunity allowances refresh even if they were used during Round 0.

Surprised, Flat-Footed, and Unseen Attackers

Surprised means that a creature is genuinely unaware of an attacker or danger. A Surprised creature receives no Movement or Main Action during Round 0.

Against an attack it has not perceived, the creature cannot choose or roll an active Dodge, Parry, or Block. Its Defense is:

10 + Base Attack Bonus (BAB) + applicable passive magical defensive modifiers

The creature rolls no d20 and receives no Dexterity modifier. It cannot spend Physical Energy (PE) to improve this Defense, suppress a defensive penalty, restore Dexterity, gain a Defense roll, or remove the static restriction. After being hit, it may still use any otherwise eligible PE option that reduces damage or prevents a Mortal Wound.

Surprise can continue after Round 0 if the creature still has not perceived the danger. Once it becomes aware but has not yet taken its first action, it is normally Flat-Footed instead.

Flat-Footed means that a creature has become aware too late to make a complete defensive response. A creature that detects a sudden, concealed, or unexpectedly fast hostile action can avoid being Surprised but remain Flat-Footed if the attacker acts before it. This state normally ends when the creature takes its first action.

A Flat-Footed creature may choose an otherwise legal Dodge, Parry, or Block, but resolves it as a static Defense:

10 + BAB + applicable bonuses for the chosen Defense + applicable magical defensive modifiers

The creature rolls no d20 and adds no Dexterity modifier. Parry may retain applicable weapon or Strength-based bonuses, Block may retain applicable shield bonuses, and Dodge retains only applicable non-Dexterity bonuses. A chosen static Dodge, Parry, or Block still consumes one use of that Defense.

A Flat-Footed creature cannot make attacks of opportunity unless an explicit ability permits it. It cannot spend PE to improve the static Defense or suppress another defensive penalty, although otherwise eligible post-hit damage-mitigation and survival options remain available.

When a creature knows an unseen attacker is present but cannot locate or anticipate that attacker, it remains Flat-Footed against that attacker for as long as the attacker remains unlocatable and unanticipatable. Taking an unrelated action does not end this attacker-specific state.

Uncanny Dodge prevents ordinary Surprise or acting later in an unexpected opening from making the character Flat-Footed. If Flat-Footed overlaps the static Parry or Block restriction caused by an attacker at least five experience levels higher or two size categories larger, use the stricter Defense:

Neither Surprise nor Flat-Footed automatically makes an attack a critical hit or Lethal Damage.

Joining Combat and Giving Warnings

A creature that is completely unaware and not yet involved does not roll initiative merely because combat is occurring elsewhere. It rolls when it is alerted, directly attacked, or otherwise drawn into the encounter.

Round 0 does not automatically alert everyone nearby. Sleeping creatures, distracted bystanders, and creatures unable to perceive a concealed attack may remain unaware after it ends.

A meaningful warning or order can alert such a creature and bring it into initiative. During Round 0, that warning consumes the speaker's action. It does not grant the newly alerted creature an action during the Round 0 already in progress. A creature made aware during a round rolls initiative to enter combat in the next round.

Initiative, Rounds, and Attack Speed

Initiative establishes when creatures act. Initiative passes distribute repeated attacks through the round, while Spell Speed schedules spell completion independently of those passes.

Initiative and Ties

When combat begins, every current participant rolls:

1d20 + Dexterity modifier + explicit initiative modifiers

The total establishes initiative order for the encounter. Strength does not affect initiative. An explicit feat, talent, spell, item, or circumstance may still modify the roll according to its own text.

When initiative totals tie, the creature with the higher initiative modifier acts first. If both the total and modifier remain tied, allied creatures may choose their order. Opponents and other non-cooperating creatures act simultaneously; neither action preempts the other.

Rounds and Initiative Passes

Combat proceeds in rounds. An initiative pass is one sweep through the initiative order, from highest to lowest.

Every participant receives a first initiative pass in an ordinary round. After all first-pass actions resolve, participants entitled to another attack pass act again in initiative order. Continue through the third, fourth, and later passes until nobody has another pass.

Attacks made on separate passes resolve sequentially. They do not become simultaneous merely because they belong to the same character's Full Attack.

Ordinary Movement and the Main Action begin on the first pass. Later passes normally supply the remaining attacks of a Full Attack. They are not additional unrestricted turns, although an explicit rule may replace an attack or permit another use.

Resolve every numeric Spell Speed completion scheduled during the current pass before beginning the next initiative pass. Do not begin the next round until every attack pass and every numeric completion scheduled for the current round has resolved.

Attack Speed and Primary Attacks

Your character's Primary Attack is their primary method of attacking. Choose it when declaring a Full Attack; it supplies that Full Attack's ordinary attacks. Unless stated otherwise, Attack-Speed increases supply attacks only with the Primary Attack.

When declaring a Full Attack, choose its Primary Attack and any optional abilities you will use. The resulting attacks form that Full Attack's schedule.

A whole-number Attack Speed grants that many attack passes every round. A half-step alternates between a higher and lower round. Attack Speed 1.5 follows 2, 1, 2, 1...; Attack Speed 2.5 follows 3, 2, 3, 2.... Every combat begins with the higher number.

A fractional cycle advances at the end of every combat round, even when the character makes no Full Attack during that round. Final Attack Speed cannot be reduced below 1.

An Attack-Speed increase granted by a particular weapon applies only to attacks made with that weapon. A generally applicable Attack-Speed increase applies to the Primary Attack unless a rule permits another allocation.

An Attack-Speed change takes effect at the start of the next round. If the new value is fractional, that round begins with the higher number.

A Full Attack can contain no more than five attacks in one round unless an effect explicitly raises that limit.

Example: Mercenary and Swashbuckler Attack Speed

Mercenary. A Mercenary wielding a two-handed mastered weapon begins with Attack Speed 1. Second Attack raises it to 1.5, Third Attack raises it to 2, and Weapon Mastery III raises it to 2.5. The Mercenary therefore makes three attacks in the first round, two in the second, then repeats that cycle. Every attack uses the two-handed Primary Attack.

Swashbuckler. A Swashbuckler with Dual Wield makes one Primary and one offhand attack in each paired pass. Second Attack grants a second paired pass, producing four attacks each round. At full Greased Lightning buildup, the Swashbuckler gains one additional unpaired attack and reaches five attacks.

Spell Speed and Scheduled Completion

Spell Speed is a delay measured down the initiative count. It is not an attack pass.

Calculate the completion count when casting begins:

completion count = initiative count when casting begins - Spell Speed

All ordinary actions assigned to the completion count finish before the spell resolves. The spell takes effect at the end of that initiative number. If several spells complete on the same count, they resolve simultaneously and all their effects occur; one does not preempt another merely because it is narrated first.

A numeric Spell Speed can continue below initiative 0 without entering another round. Count every integer, including initiative numbers on which no creature acts. Casting times written in full rounds, minutes, hours, or another unit follow their stated duration instead.

The caster pays the spell's full Magical Energy when casting begins, and the spell remains in progress until it completes. Spellcasting in Combat governs interruption, failed spells, and invalid targets.

Example: Spell Speed Across Initiative Counts

A Speed 3 spell begun at initiative 25 is scheduled for initiative 22. Every ordinary action at 22 resolves first, then the spell completes at the end of that count. A Speed 2 spell begun at initiative 24 also completes at the end of 22, so both spells resolve simultaneously. If a Speed 30 spell begins at initiative 5, it completes at initiative -25 during the same round; passing initiative 0 does not move a numeric Spell Speed into the next round.

Delay, Ready, and Pre-Combat Preparation

Waiting and Delay. When the character's first initiative pass arrives, the character may Delay the entire turn instead of acting and re-enter later in the initiative order. Individual later attack passes cannot be delayed separately. Delay cannot interrupt an action already in progress. Once the character acts, that later position becomes the character's initiative for the rest of the encounter.

Delay never grants an additional turn. If the character delays into the next round and acts before the former initiative position returns, that delayed action is the character's turn for the new round.

Ready. A character may Ready only after initiative has begun. Ready consumes the Main Action and prepares one eligible Main Action against an observable trigger. The character must state both the prepared action and its trigger. One weapon attack, beginning a spell, or another eligible Main Action may be prepared; a Full Attack cannot be readied.

If the trigger occurs before the character's next turn, the prepared action begins immediately before the triggering action. The triggering creature resumes afterward if still able. Resolving the readied action moves the readying character's initiative immediately before the triggering creature for the rest of the encounter. If the trigger does not occur before the character's next turn, the prepared action is lost.

A readied action retains all its ordinary costs, risks, and consequences. It may still provoke an attack of opportunity. Ready grants no separate Movement.

Readied spells. A readied spell is not cast in advance and held. Casting begins when the trigger occurs: the caster pays its Magical Energy and starts its normal Spell Speed countdown. The casting begins before the triggering action, but a spell with nonzero Spell Speed may finish after that action and other later events.

Preparation before initiative. An attack or action prepared before combat is not a Readied Action and grants no automatic free action before initiative. Current participants still roll initiative. Credible preparation may receive a situational initiative bonus or affect whether Surprise Round 0 occurs, as decided by the DM. When the prepared action occurs, it consumes the character's normal Main Action.

Drawing or bringing a weapon to hand is equipment handling, not the formal Ready action described here.

If Delay or Ready changes the initiative of a character who shared an initiative position through a formation or group rule, that character leaves the former shared position.

Actions in Combat

Each character normally receives one Movement and one Main Action each round. Movement determines whether the character moves or handles equipment. The Main Action determines what the character does: make a single attack, begin a Full Attack, cast a spell, or perform another legal action. Additional initiative passes from Attack Speed belong to the same Full Attack; they are not additional unrestricted turns.

Movement and Main Actions

Movement and the Main Action are separate allowances. Movement does not consume an attack, and using the Main Action does not ordinarily consume Movement.

A single attack, a Full Attack, spellcasting, and other ordinary Main Actions are alternatives. Choosing one gives up the others unless an explicit rule permits a combination.

Movement and the Main Action normally begin during the character's first initiative pass. Later passes provide only the unresolved attacks of a Full Attack or another use explicitly permitted during an attack pass.

Physical Energy Costs and Reaching Zero

Physical Energy cannot fall below 0. A character may use an action, maneuver, response, or ability with a PE cost only when able to pay the full cost.

Add together every PE cost within the same action or sequence. For example, a Full Attack containing Hard Trip costs 1 PE for the Full Attack and 2 PE for Hard Trip, for a total of 3 PE. There is no general limit on how much PE a character may spend during one round.

An action that spends the character's final PE resolves completely. Immediately afterward, the character becomes Exhausted. If that action is a Full Attack, its continuing sequence resolves normally unless something else interrupts it; Exhausted begins when that sequence ends.

Exhausted sets and locks PE at 0. Each round, the character may take either half Movement or one Main Action, never both. That Main Action may be one ordinary attack, a spell, or another action that costs no PE; it cannot be a Full Attack. The character cannot Run, Charge, or use any PE-costing option, and takes -6

Single Attacks and Full Attacks

A single attack uses the Main Action to make exactly one attack, regardless of the character's Attack Speed. A single attack has no general PE cost.

A Full Attack is a Main Action that resolves every attack in the character's current Full-Attack schedule, subject to the Full-Attack limit. Its attacks resolve sequentially on the character's scheduled initiative passes. A Full Attack is one continuing action, not a separate Main Action on every pass.

A Full Attack always costs 1 PE total, whether it produces one attack or many. A character unable to pay that cost may still make a legal single attack. Heavy and two-handed weapons may make either kind of attack normally.

Movement Before and After Attacks

A character making a single attack may complete Movement before or after the attack. Movement cannot be divided around that attack unless an explicit rule permits it.

A character making a Full Attack must complete all ordinary Movement before the first attack. Once the Full Attack begins, the character cannot use ordinary Movement afterward or during a later pass. A character who wishes to attack and then move must choose a single attack.

An explicit ability may override these restrictions according to its own text.

Replacing Attacks During a Full Attack

A Full Attack normally contains only melee or ranged weapon attacks. An unused attack cannot be converted freely into inventory use, spellcasting, conversation, or another unrelated action.

An action may replace one or more attacks only when a maneuver, talent, spell, item, equipment feature, or other explicit rule permits it during a Full Attack. Pay its listed attack cost from the attacks that remain unused that round. If an option supplies no Full-Attack substitution procedure, it cannot be inserted into the sequence.

A permitted substitution may occur on any chosen pass unless its own timing says otherwise. Several different substitutions may be used during the same round when each is permitted, every cost is paid, and every usage limit is obeyed.

The DM may allow a narrow exception for something immediately combat-related, physically local, and sensible within the attack sequence. Picking up a weapon at the character's feet may cost one attack. Retrieving something that requires Movement is unavailable after the Full Attack begins. Drinking a potion requires it to be already held or made immediately accessible by suitable equipment.

A dual-wielding Swashbuckler's ordinary pass contains both weapon attacks as one paired pass. Replacing that pass forfeits both attacks; neither hand can pay a substitution cost independently. A genuinely unpaired extra attack may pay a one-attack cost on its own.

Casting a spell normally uses the entire Main Action. Combat Casting or another explicit permission is required to combine spellcasting with melee attacks in the same round.

Later Passes and Changing Targets

Later Full-Attack passes are not locked to the first target. On a later pass, the attacker may choose any legal target that can be attacked from the character's current position, including a target within current melee reach or legal ranged range.

Changing targets grants no Movement. If the original target dies, teleports, moves away, or otherwise becomes unavailable and no other target can be legally attacked from the current position, the remaining attacks are wasted.

Drawing, Dropping, and Putting Away Equipment

Bringing one Accessible item to hand uses Movement. An item in a pack or ordinary closed container is Stored rather than Accessible; a locked, tied-down, buried, or deeply packed item is Secured. Equipment defines those access states and the additional handling they require.

A character with BAB +1 or higher may bring any one Accessible item to hand while using ordinary Movement, then use the Main Action normally. A character without BAB +1 spends the Movement allowance to bring the item to hand and gains no movement from that allowance. Drawing an Accessible weapon follows this general procedure.

Dropping an item held in hand is free.

Sheathing, holstering, or otherwise putting away a weapon uses Movement and provokes an attack of opportunity while threatened. Drawing an accessible weapon does not provoke.

Bows and crossbows use the same drawing procedure. Loading, cocking, ammunition retention, and other weapon-specific handling are described by their Equipment rules.

An explicit Quick Draw permission allows its named weapon to be drawn without spending Movement. It does not accelerate sheathing or another equipment action unless its own text says so.

Drawing a weapon is equipment handling. It is not the Ready action and establishes no trigger.

Movement and Position

Movement determines where a character can act, which enemies can be reached, and who threatens whom. Unless an explicit rule says otherwise, ordinary Movement occurs during the character's first initiative pass.

Speed, Squares, and Diagonal Movement

Speed is the distance a character can cover with ordinary Movement. An ordinary Medium humanoid has Speed 30 feet.

When a grid is used, each square represents 5 feet. Count diagonal movement by alternating 5 feet, then 10 feet, repeating for each diagonal step along the path. The rules still operate in feet and do not require a grid.

Size alone does not determine Speed. A race, creature, mount, spell, condition, or item states any value that differs from the ordinary Speed above.

A character who still has both Movement and the Main Action may spend both on ordinary movement during the first initiative pass to move up to twice Speed. This double movement grants no special protection from attacks of opportunity. It does not allow a character limited to one action to exchange that action for twice the normal distance.

Difficult Terrain and Occupied Spaces

Each foot crossed through difficult terrain costs twice as much Movement. Difficult terrain prevents Run and Charge unless an explicit rule says otherwise.

A character may move through a willing ally's space but cannot ordinarily end Movement there. Moving through an enemy's space requires a legal maneuver, an explicit ability, or situational permission from the DM.

Vanaheim has no general five-foot step. A character cannot use a small free movement to avoid the ordinary consequences of leaving a threatened space.

Run, Charge, and Withdraw

Run. Running consumes both Movement and the Main Action. The character moves up to four times Speed along a reasonably straight, unobstructed route. Heavy armor or being Encumbered reduces this to three times Speed.

A running character loses the Dexterity modifier from Defense until the character's next turn. A character may Run for consecutive rounds equal to the Constitution score without an additional cost. Each further consecutive round costs 1 PE. Spending the final PE completes that round's Run before the character becomes Exhausted. A Fatigued or Exhausted character cannot Run.

An explicit Run ability may change the multiplier or allow the character to retain Dexterity according to its own text.

Charge. Charging consumes both Movement and the Main Action. The character moves at least 10 feet and up to twice Speed along a straight, clear route toward a target, then makes one melee attack. Difficult terrain or another obstruction prevents an ordinary Charge.

The attack gains +2 attack, and the charger takes -2 Defense until the charger's next turn. Charge movement can provoke attacks of opportunity. A Charge never produces a Full Attack unless an explicit ability says otherwise. A Fatigued or Exhausted character cannot Charge.

Withdraw. Withdrawing consumes both Movement and the Main Action and allows movement up to twice Speed. Against visible opponents, treat the character's starting space as unthreatened for this movement. Leaving any later threatened space still provokes normally.

An unseen opponent may still make an attack of opportunity because the withdrawing character could not deliberately avoid that threat. A Blinded character cannot Withdraw.

A character limited to one action may use that action to Withdraw up to the character's current Speed.

Reach and Threatened Areas

Reach is the distance at which a creature can make a melee attack. A creature threatens every space it can presently attack in melee, even outside its own turn.

Small and Medium creatures ordinarily have 5-foot reach. A Large creature ordinarily has 10-foot reach, subject to its body shape and individual statistics.

A creature without a weapon, natural attack, or an explicit ability that treats its unarmed attacks as armed does not normally threaten an area.

Attacks of Opportunity

An attack of opportunity is an immediate melee attack against an enemy who leaves an opening while threatened. Making one is voluntary.

The following general actions provoke:

  • moving out of a space threatened by an enemy; entering a threatened space does not provoke;
  • making a ranged weapon attack while threatened;
  • making an unarmed attack against an armed target when no rule treats the attacker as armed;
  • beginning to cast a spell or use a spell-like ability while threatened, unless a defensive-casting rule prevents the opportunity;
  • drinking a potion, applying an oil, or reading a scroll while threatened;
  • standing from Prone while threatened;
  • Running while threatened;
  • trying to control a frightened mount while threatened;
  • helping another creature perform an action that would itself provoke while threatened;
  • using Medicine to stabilize another creature, or using another skill that requires comparable sustained attention, while threatened;
  • retrieving a stored item, picking up an item, moving a heavy object, sheathing or holstering a weapon, loading a crossbow, lighting a torch, preparing a splash weapon, or performing comparable distracting equipment handling while threatened; and
  • missing with a Heavy Attack made with a Heavy Weapon wielded in both hands, which provokes only from the opponent who successfully defended, or failing an untrained Hard Trip or Bash, as defined by those maneuvers; and
  • performing any action whose own rule states that it provokes.

Bringing an Accessible item to hand, dropping a held item, speaking briefly, Delaying, declaring Ready, and making an ordinary melee attack or Full Attack do not themselves provoke. A readied action still provokes when the action it executes normally would.

A specific action, maneuver, item, or ability may add, remove, or change a trigger. Combat maneuvers do not provoke merely because they are maneuvers: each maneuver's own entry controls. Disarm failure does not otherwise provoke, and the defender's free attack against some Grab attempts is a separate procedure rather than an attack of opportunity.

Resolve the attack of opportunity immediately, before the triggering action or movement finishes. Afterward, the interrupted action continues if it remains possible.

The attack is one ordinary melee attack resolved through the normal attack, active Defense, Margin of Success, damage, and protection rules. It consumes one attack-of-opportunity allowance, not Movement, the Main Action, or a scheduled attack pass.

A character normally has one attack-of-opportunity allowance per round, regardless of how many opportunities occur. An explicit ability may grant additional allowances. A character may use the allowance even after attacking during the character's own turn.

Even with additional allowances, one triggering opportunity permits only one attack. Separate triggers may permit separate attacks when the character still has allowances available.

Moving out of several spaces threatened by the same enemy during one continuous movement creates only one opportunity for that enemy. Different enemies may each respond if they threaten the movement and possess an available allowance.

A Flat-Footed character cannot make attacks of opportunity unless an explicit ability permits it. Uncanny Dodge permits otherwise legal attacks of opportunity before the character's first action.

There is no five-foot-step exception to these rules.

Static Dodge = 10 + BAB + Dexterity modifier + applicable passive magical defensive modifiers

Relevant cover still applies. A passive ability contributes only when its own text applies to that attack; static Dodge does not create additional class benefits.

Attacking and Defending

An attack is resolved against one chosen Defense. The attacker declares what is being attempted, the defender commits to a legal response, and the two totals determine whether the attack connects and how cleanly it lands.

Whenever a defender is permitted to choose a Defense, they may decline an active Defense and use Passive Defense:

10 + BAB + applicable passive magical defensive modifiers

Passive Defense is chosen before either combat roll. It rolls no d20 and spends no Dodge, Parry, or Block use. A condition that permits no defensive choice and supplies a stricter value, such as Helpless, still uses its own procedure. A Golem Frame uses the profile-based Passive Defense defined by its own subsystem.

Declaring and Resolving an Attack

Resolve an ordinary attack in this order:

  1. The attacker declares the target and describes the attack clearly enough for the defender to understand what is coming.
  1. The defender chooses an otherwise legal Dodge, Parry, or Block, or uses Passive Defense, before either combat roll is made. The defender does not see the attack die or final Attack total before choosing.
  1. Roll the Attack and any chosen active Defense, or use Passive Defense or the appropriate static Defense.
  1. If Attack is lower than Defense, the attack misses. If Attack meets or exceeds Defense, it connects.
  1. Calculate the Margin of Success and pass the result to Damage and Protection.

The physical attack determines which defenses are legal. A shield can answer some attacks that cannot reasonably be parried, and Dodge requires somewhere to evade.

Attack Rolls and Natural Results

An Attack roll is:

1d20 + BAB + relevant ability modifier + applicable feat, talent, magical, weapon, and situational modifiers

Ordinary melee attacks normally use Strength, and ordinary ranged attacks normally use Dexterity. A weapon, attack, feat, talent, spell, or other explicit rule may use a different ability.

Apply a modifier only when it is relevant to the attack being made. A bonus written only for damage is not an Attack modifier, and a bonus written only for a particular weapon, method, or circumstance does not apply outside it.

A natural 1 on the Attack die is an automatic miss.

Dodge, Parry, and Block

Dodge is:

1d20 + BAB + Dexterity modifier + applicable Dodge and situational modifiers

Dodge requires enough physical room to evade the attack and can be penalized or denied by the situation.

Parry uses the character's Attack calculation with the parrying weapon, retaining only modifiers relevant to defense. Offensive-only bonuses do not apply. Defensive bonuses and penalties do. A character without a suitable weapon has no ordinary Parry.

Block is:

1d20 + BAB + Strength modifier + shield bonus + applicable defensive modifiers

Use the shield's Block value, or its Projectile Block value against a qualifying projectile. Applicable defensive modifiers include bonuses from Enhanced Block, Weapon Focus (Shields), shield material, and magical Accuracy. Add each bonus once, accounting for any material bonus already included in the shield's printed profile.

An explicit feat, talent, or ability may substitute Dexterity or another ability for Strength. A character without a suitable shield has no ordinary Block. A shield may Block some attacks that cannot reasonably be Parried.

An explicit unarmed-defense ability may permit defenses without a weapon or shield according to its own rules.

Margin of Success

When an attack connects, calculate:

Margin of Success = Attack - Defense

Attack equal to Defense is a hit with a Margin of Success of 0. Add the final Margin directly to the attack's damage under Damage and Protection.

An attack-only bonus can therefore improve damage indirectly by increasing Margin of Success. Do not add it to damage a second time unless its own rule also grants an explicit damage bonus.

Advantage, Disadvantage, and Rerolls

Advantage means rolling 2d20 and keeping the higher die. Disadvantage means rolling 2d20 and keeping the lower die. The rule granting Advantage or Disadvantage identifies which roll it affects; it does not automatically apply to every related d20 roll.

The DM may assign Advantage or Disadvantage when circumstances materially favor or hinder a roll. Neither makes an impossible action possible; the DM may rule that an attempt simply cannot succeed.

Count Advantage and Disadvantage sources against one another one for one. Equal numbers cancel completely. If any Advantage remains, roll with Advantage once. If any Disadvantage remains, roll with Disadvantage once. Additional uncancelled sources do not add more dice.

A reroll applied to an advantaged or disadvantaged roll normally rerolls one chosen die from the two-die pool. Replace that die, then determine the

result from the revised pool. An explicit reroll ability may state a different procedure.

A character with an available Luck reroll may spend it after seeing one of their own d20 results. Reroll and keep the result more favorable to the character. On an advantaged or disadvantaged roll, reroll one chosen die, resolve the revised pool, then keep the more favorable of the original and revised results.

Before making a disadvantaged roll, the character may instead spend one Luck reroll to cancel the remaining Disadvantage and roll one ordinary d20. Luck rerolls are a limited per-story-arc resource. Damage dice and other non-d20 rolls are not universally eligible.

Weapon Configurations and Two-Weapon Fighting

Weapon configurations are physical equipment choices. Each configuration grants only the benefits listed below and in the relevant equipment rules.

  • Wielding one one-handed weapon grants no universal numerical bonus. Its benefit is leaving the other hand free.
  • Wielding a weapon and shield grants the ordinary properties of that weapon and shield, including separate Parry and Block uses. The arrangement grants no additional implicit modifier.
  • Wielding a two-handed or Heavy Weapon grants only that weapon's written properties and access to Heavy Attack when its requirements are met. It receives no universal Attack Speed, Parry, or mandatory-Full-Attack penalty for being Heavy.
  • Any further benefit requires an explicit weapon, feat, talent, class feature, or technique.

When fighting with a weapon in each hand without Dual Wield, choose one as the Primary Attack. The second weapon grants one separate, unpaired offhand attack pass every two rounds. Place that pass in the round with fewer Primary attacks; if both rounds contain the same number, place it in the first round.

Attacks and Parries made with either weapon take -4. If the offhand weapon is Light, this penalty becomes -2. Dodge is unaffected.

Each suitable weapon supplies one base Parry use made with that weapon. A single-attack Main Action still makes only one attack.

A character with Dual Wield uses that Talent's procedure instead.

An ordinary bare unarmed attack uses the normal melee Attack roll with Strength. Magic Protection and Magic Resistance

Magic Protection (MP) is a passive, flat reduction against spell damage. Subtract the target's MP from applicable spell damage. For example, MP 4 reduces 10 spell damage to 6.

MP is not Magical Energy. It cannot be spent, reserved, recovered, drained as payment, or used as an ability cost. Magical costs and recovery use Magical Energy (ME).

Magic Resistance (MR) is a percentile chance to resist magic completely. When MR applies, roll percentile dice. A result within the listed percentage succeeds; for

example, MR 20% succeeds on 01-20. Successful MR completely resists the magic for that creature.

MR is not a saving throw and does not add to one. MP, MR, and saving throws remain separate defenses. A successful MR check leaves no spell damage for MP to reduce; when MR does not resist the magic, resolve any applicable saving throw and MP normally.

Ranged Combat

Ranged attacks use the ordinary Attack and Defense procedure, modified by the weapon's Equipment range bands, nearby allies, cover, concealment, and the weapon being used.

Ranged Attacks and Defenses

A ranged weapon attack normally uses Dexterity as its relevant Attack ability. The weapon must be ready, the target must be within the weapon's maximum range, and total physical cover must not prevent direct targeting. The Equipment chapter defines the weapon's Effective, Far, and Extreme distance bands. Apply the appropriate band, firing-into-melee, cover, and other relevant modifiers before resolving the ordinary Attack and Defense rolls.

Dodge can defend against a projectile when physical evasion is possible. A suitable shield can Block ordinary arrows, bolts, and comparable projectiles. Parrying a projectile requires an explicit ability or exceptional permission.

Making a ranged weapon attack while threatened provokes an attack of opportunity. Resolve the attack of opportunity immediately before the ranged attack, then resume the ranged attack if it remains possible.

A ranged Full Attack follows the ordinary Full Attack rules. Drawing a bow or crossbow follows the ordinary weapon-drawing procedure. Ammunition, loading, cocking, each weapon's range, and other weapon-specific handling belong to its Equipment entry.

Firing into Melee

Firing or throwing at a target engaged in melee with the attacker's ally imposes -4 Attack.

  • Reduce the penalty to -2 when the target is two size categories larger than the nearest ally.
  • Remove the penalty when the target is at least three size categories larger than the nearest ally.
  • Remove the penalty when the aimed target, or the part of the target being aimed at, is at least 10 feet from the nearest ally.
  • Precise Shot removes the penalty according to its own text.

An ordinary miss while firing into melee does not create a random attack against an ally.

Damage and Protection

Damage first meets protection, then passes through the target's protective pools toward actual bodily harm. This section uses six related terms:

  • Raw damage is the complete damage total before any protection is applied.
  • Damage Reduction (DR) is a numeric protection subtracted from damage when that protection applies.
  • Armor Penetration (Pen) measures an attack's ability to defeat physical armor through piercing, cutting, crushing, or overwhelming force.
  • Armor Grade (Grade) measures how difficult physical armor is to defeat before applying that armor's listed DR.
  • Hit Points (HP) are one protective pool increased by Jobs, training, worn armor, and other explicit effects. Temporary HP is separate only when its source says so.
  • Life Points (LP) represent the character's actual bodily survival. Lethal Damage bypasses HP and reaches LP directly, but applicable DR and Physical Energy mitigation still resolve first.

Calculating Damage

Ordinary weapon damage is:

weapon dice + relevant ability modifier + explicit damage modifiers + Margin of Success

Use the ability modifier specified by the weapon or attack. Margin of Success is the amount by which the Attack total met or exceeded the Defense; a tied Attack and Defense therefore add 0 damage from Margin while still producing a hit.

A bonus that applies only to the Attack roll can increase damage indirectly by increasing Margin of Success. Do not add it directly to damage a second time unless its source explicitly grants a damage bonus as well.

Size supplies no separate universal damage modifier. Larger creatures normally express their power through attributes, larger weapon dice, or explicit abilities. Weapon dice and the weapon-size progression belong to Equipment.

A Heavy Attack is a special maneuver made only with a Heavy Weapon wielded in both hands. It normally consumes two attacks to deliver one blow; its complete action and damage procedure appears under Combat Maneuvers, while its interaction with physical protection is defined below.

Damage Resolution Order

Resolve each damage result in this order:

  1. Calculate raw damage, including weapon dice, the relevant ability modifier, explicit damage modifiers, Margin of Success, and any critical or other dice modification.
  1. Apply and combine all applicable DR after resolving any Armor Penetration, Armor Grade, material, Heavy Attack, magical, supernatural, or critical adjustments. Ordinary DR can reduce damage to 0.
  1. Once the post-DR result is known, the target may apply one eligible PE damage-mitigation option.
  1. Route the remaining damage through any applicable Temporary HP, then HP and LP. If the damage is Lethal, route it directly to LP instead.

Actual damage is the final amount remaining after DR and any eligible PE mitigation that is routed into HP or LP. It includes damage absorbed by HP and is not limited to damage that reaches LP.

Unless a rule explicitly states otherwise, round fractions down at every stage.

When several ordinary multipliers apply to the same base value, start at x1, then add the amount by which each multiplier exceeds x1.

Two x2 multipliers therefore become x3, while x4 combined with x3 becomes x6. An explicit rule may define a separate operation instead.

Hit Points and Life Points

A character's base LP equals the Constitution score, plus any explicit racial or class LP bonuses. Jobs, classes, purchased training, worn armor, and other sources all contribute to the character's single HP pool.

If the same final result were Lethal Damage, it would bypass both HP pools and remove 8 LP instead.

Nonlethal Damage

Nonlethal damage, sometimes called subdual damage, follows the ordinary damage sequence through HP and LP. It causes no Wound when it reaches LP. A creature reduced to 0 LP by nonlethal damage becomes Unconscious instead of dying.

Lethal Damage

Lethal Damage follows the normal DR and PE-mitigation sequence, then bypasses Temporary HP and HP and applies directly to LP.

A deliberate attack that genuinely connects with a bound, sleeping, or otherwise helpless body ordinarily deals Lethal Damage. A surprise killing strike may also be Lethal when the attacker's training, method, and circumstances support it. Ordinary combat sneak attacks are not automatically Lethal, and Surprise or Flat-Footed status alone is insufficient. A deliberately nonlethal method may remain HP damage when that result is physically plausible.

Superior level or size does not automatically make an attack Lethal. Falling damage is an exception to the ordinary sequence: it ignores DR, may still be reduced by eligible PE mitigation, then bypasses HP and reaches LP. An overwhelming projectile may be Lethal when the DM judges that its actual effect warrants it.

The DM classifies Lethal Damage from the physical circumstances, attacker competence, declared intent, and nature of the attack. Armor and magical defenses can influence whether that classification makes sense; once damage is classified as Lethal, HP does not protect against it.

Critical Hits

An attack that connects within its critical threat range is a critical hit. There is no confirmation roll. A natural 20 must still connect and is not an automatic hit.

Determine the attack's complete damage-dice expression, apply the four-damage-dice conversion, roll the remaining dice once, and multiply the entire damage-dice subtotal by 2. Converted +2 values remain part of that subtotal. Do not roll a second set. Add non-dice modifiers such as Strength and Margin of Success only once.

Apply critical multiplication after rolling the damage dice. First determine the ordinary weapon dice after effects that change their number, such as a straight, clear mounted charge of at least 30 feet made with an appropriate weapon; convert every damage die beyond the fourth to +2, roll the remaining dice once, include those converted values, then multiply that subtotal by 2.

Critical hits bypass most DR; any applicable magical DR is halved. A critical hit does not bypass HP by default. It reaches LP directly only when an explicit critical result or an independent Lethal-Damage classification says so.

A critical hit may cause a deadly or lasting wound when the circumstances or an explicit result require one.

Example: Critical Damage and Multiple Multipliers

Two ordinary x2 multipliers applied to the same value combine into x3, not x4. Effects that change the number of weapon dice happen before a critical instead. A qualifying mounted charge changes a 1d8 weapon to 2d8; if those dice roll 11 on a critical hit, double that one rolled result to 22, then add +3 Strength and +4 Margin once for 29 damage. Do not double the Strength or Margin.

Wounds, Dying, and Death

Injury is tracked through three separate systems:

  • An LP wound band measures the severity of the character's current positive LP and supplies wound penalties.
  • A Mortal Wound is a distinct, lasting injury tracked separately from LP. Active Mortal Wounds count toward their own death limit.
  • Bleeding is an independently tracked source of continuing LP loss.

These systems interact only where a rule explicitly says that they do. A Mortal Wound does not automatically cause Bleeding, Bleeding does not automatically cause a Mortal Wound, and restoring LP does not by itself remove either one.

Wound Bands and Wound Penalties

Count the character's positive LP downward from maximum LP and divide it into three bands. Assign half the maximum LP to Light, rounding up. Divide the remaining points between Serious and Critical, assigning any extra point to Serious.

Current wound bandPortion of positive LPEffect
LightFirst half, counted downward from maximum LPNo wound penalty.
SeriousNext quarter-2 wound penalty.

The Serious and Critical values are total penalties for the current band; they do not stack with one another. Apply the current wound penalty to Attack rolls, rolled active Defenses, Reflex saves, ability checks, skill checks, and maneuver checks. Do not

apply it to Fortitude saves, Will saves, damage rolls, DCs, DR, or static values.

The Critical-band Attack Speed penalty cannot reduce Primary Attack Speed below the general minimum of 1. Exactly 0 LP is outside all three positive wound bands.

Example: Dividing an Odd Life Point Total

A character with maximum LP 17 assigns 9 points to Light, leaving 8. Those remaining points divide evenly into 4 Serious and 4 Critical. The exact bands are therefore Light at 17 through 9 LP, Serious at 8 through 5, and Critical at 4 through 1. At 4 LP the character takes the total -4 wound penalty, cannot Run, and loses 0.5 Primary Attack Speed; an Attack Speed of 1 remains 1 because it cannot fall below the general minimum.

Zero and Negative Life Points

At exactly 0 LP, or at any survivable negative LP value, a character becomes Unconscious. Negative LP is the Deadly wound band; exactly 0 is neither a positive wound band nor part of the Deadly band.

The character is Helpless: they fall Prone, drop everything held, cannot move, speak, act, or make active Defenses, and use this static Defense:

10 + applicable passive magical defensive modifiers

Do not add BAB, Dexterity, weapon or shield bonuses, or another active-defense benefit. The complete Unconscious condition appears under Combat Conditions.

Spellcasting in Combat

Any character able to cast a spell may do so during combat. Ordinary spellcasting uses the Main Action and cannot be combined with a Full Attack. Combat Casting is the established procedure for combining a spell with later melee attacks in the same round and for gaining its other listed protections.

Individual spells define their own Magical Energy costs, targets, ranges, areas, durations, components, saving throws, and effects. This section governs the shared combat procedure: paying for a spell, completing or losing it, casting while threatened, and casting while involved in a grapple.

Casting a Spell and Paying Magical Energy

Spell costs use Magical Energy (ME), never Magic Protection (MP). Before beginning, the caster must have the spell's required components—its listed speech, gestures, or materials—and enough unoccupied Concentration capacity, the Intelligence-modifier limit on committed spell focus. Both are detailed below. Declare the spell and its target or area as its rules require, then pay its full ME cost when casting begins.

Once casting begins, schedule and resolve the spell under Spell Speed and Scheduled Completion. The spell remains in progress until that completion. A readied spell begins and pays its ME only when its trigger occurs, as explained under Delay, Ready, and Pre-Combat Preparation.

A caster who cannot pay the full ME cost cannot ordinarily begin the spell. As a last resort, the DM may permit voluntary spending into negative ME and may require a Will save. Each complete 2 ME reached below zero causes 1 LP damage: reaching -1 ME causes none, reaching -2 causes 1 LP, and reaching -4 causes another 1 LP. Such use may permanently damage the caster's maximum ME at the DM's discretion. Ordinary ME-draining effects stop at 0 unless they explicitly say otherwise.

If the spell's target dies or otherwise becomes invalid before completion, the spell is wasted. It produces no effect, and all ME paid for it remains spent.

Spell Interruption and Failed Spells

The casting interval begins when the caster pays the spell's ME and ends when the spell completes. A successful hit interrupts casting only if at least 1 actual damage remains after DR and PE mitigation. If either reduces the result to 0, the spell is not interrupted. A missed or successfully defended attack likewise does not interrupt.

A successful Grab—the non-damaging initiation that establishes grapple control—against a caster during this interval is also a physical interruption. Other effects interrupt only when their own rule or the circumstances establish that they physically disrupt the casting.

  • Without Combat Casting, an interrupting hit or successful Grab automatically ruins the spell. No check is made.
  • When casting through Combat Casting, either trigger requires a d20 roll equal to or below half the caster's Wisdom score. A fractional half is effectively rounded down because the roll is a whole number. Success preserves the spell; failure ruins it.

Another physically disruptive effect follows its own explicit interruption rule or DM adjudication.

A ruined or failed spell produces no effect. Its full ME cost remains spent. A Readied Action can interrupt a spell only if its attack or effect actually reaches the caster during the casting interval.

Damage and Maintained Spells

Concentration capacity is not itself a check, but actual damage can break spells occupying that capacity. Whenever at least 1 actual damage from one result reaches a caster's Temporary HP, HP, or LP, every spell that caster is maintaining through Concentration is at risk. Damage reduced to 0 creates no check and ends no maintained spell.

Without Combat Casting, the qualifying damage ends all such maintained spells. A character who knows Combat Casting instead rolls d20 equal to or below half Wisdom for each qualifying damage result. Success preserves the maintained spells; failure ends them. This preservation roll does not require beginning Combat Casting's 4-PE combined sequence.

This is separate from interruption during the casting interval. Damage cannot retroactively ruin a completed spell, but it can end a completed spell that remains active through maintained Concentration.

Defensive Casting

Beginning an ordinary spell while threatened provokes an attack of opportunity as described under Attacks of Opportunity. The caster begins the spell and pays its costs before those attacks resolve. A successful hit from an attack of opportunity can therefore interrupt the spell normally.

Combat Casting permits the caster to attempt Defensive Casting instead:

  1. Declare Defensive Casting before beginning the spell.
  1. Pay Combat Casting's 4 PE and the spell's full ME cost.
  1. Roll d20 equal to or below the caster's adjusted Intelligence score.

On success, beginning that spell does not provoke an attack of opportunity. On failure, the spell is lost and its ME remains spent; it does not continue as an ordinary provoking cast. The paid PE is not refunded. Defensive Casting prevents only the opportunity attack caused by

beginning the spell. A later hit during the casting interval can still interrupt it.

The Defensive Casting check uses the caster's Intelligence score. It is separate from the half-Wisdom roll used to preserve a Combat Casting spell after a hit and from the Intelligence modifier used for Concentration capacity.

Combat Casting

Combat Casting costs 4 PE total for its combined casting-and-attack sequence, in addition to the spell's ME cost. Do not add the ordinary 1-PE Full Attack cost. Combat Casting is the established procedure for casting a spell and making melee attacks in the same round; another explicit ability may state its own exception.

Use the following sequence:

  1. Complete any ordinary Movement before casting. The caster cannot cast, move, and then make the later attacks.
  1. If the caster intends to make follow-up attacks, they must be within melee reach when the casting sequence begins.
  1. Use the first initiative pass to begin the spell, paying Combat Casting's 4 PE and the spell's ME.
  1. Choose one qualifying one-handed melee weapon when the sequence begins. Each of its remaining attack passes that round may be used for one melee attack with that weapon.

Combat Casting does not create attack passes. If the caster has no later pass, it grants no follow-up attack.

The Combat Casting procedure also permits Defensive Casting, changes successful-hit interruption from automatic failure to the half-Wisdom check, protects maintained spells through the same half-Wisdom target, and supplies the somatic-component exception described below while controlling a grapple or being Grappled. It does not grant an additional spell.

Components, Concentration, and Grappling

Spell components use these letters:

  • V — Verbal: the caster must be able to speak.
  • S — Somatic: the caster must be able to perform the spell's gestures.
  • M — Material: the caster must have access to and use the listed material, commonly from a component satchel. The material is expended only when the component or spell says so.

Concentration capacity measures how much spell focus a caster can commit at once. Capacity is not itself an interruption check, although actual damage can break maintained spells under Damage and Maintained Spells above. Capacity equals the caster's Intelligence modifier, and a spell writes its requirement as initial/maintenance:

  • The first number is the unoccupied capacity required to begin casting.
  • The second number is the capacity that remains occupied while the spell is maintained.
  • Do not add the two numbers during casting. The capacity that meets the initial requirement can immediately become the maintained capacity.
  • 1/- requires 1 free capacity to cast and occupies none afterward.
  • A bare entry such as 1, or an entry written as 1/, is read as 1/- unless an explicit rule supplies a maintenance requirement.

For example, a caster with Intelligence 12 has Concentration capacity 1. After casting and maintaining a 1/1 spell, that capacity is occupied and the caster cannot begin another spell requiring 1 free capacity until it becomes available.

A caster may voluntarily release one or more maintained spells as a Free Action during that caster's own turn, never off-turn as a reaction. Unconsciousness, death, or becoming Stunned ends all maintained spells. Paralysis alone does not. If Concentration capacity falls below the amount occupied, enough maintained spells end to fit the new capacity. A spell, condition, or other explicit rule may provide a more specific ending condition.

In a grapple, the Controller maintains the hold and the Grappled target is held; the complete procedure appears under Grab and Grapple. Without Combat Casting, either participant may cast only a spell that requires no somatic component. Combat Casting permits the caster to omit the somatic component while controlling a grapple or while Grappled, but it does not waive verbal or material components. A grapple option, the spell's own text, or another condition may impose further restrictions.

A Grab that establishes control over a spell already in progress uses the interruption procedure above. Being a Controller or Grappled does not create a separate Intelligence check to maintain a spell.

Combat Maneuvers

Combat maneuvers use attacks, leverage, and trained techniques to do more than inflict ordinary weapon damage. The relevant feat, talent, or class feature determines whether a character is trained in a maneuver. This section supplies the shared resolution procedure; Equipment determines weapon properties, and Talents and Feats determines access to trained use.

Roll-Under and Opposed Checks

Skills — Attribute Checks and Skill Checks owns the universal roll-under procedure, while Skills — Opposed Checks and Check Margin owns the general opposed-check and check-margin procedure. The compact calculation below is retained here so the Combat maneuvers can be resolved in place. A maneuver's stated threshold, BAB contribution, cost, result, and exceptional margin remain specific to that maneuver.

Disarm, Hard Trip, and Bash use roll-under checks based on raw ability scores rather than ability modifiers. Build the threshold named by the maneuver:

adjusted threshold = relevant ability score + BAB when stated + applicable modifiers

Roll d20. A result equal to or below the adjusted threshold succeeds. Bonuses raise the threshold; penalties and difficulty lower it. Natural 1 and natural 20 have no separate automatic effect on a roll-under check; compare the die with the threshold normally.

When the rules say to beat an ability check, they mean to roll equal to or below this threshold. Routine actions without meaningful pressure may require no roll, and an unmodified threshold of 20 cannot fail an ordinary d20 check.

The result's check margin is:

check margin = adjusted threshold - d20 result

In an unopposed check, a margin of 0 or more succeeds. In an opposed check, both participants roll and the higher check margin wins, even when both margins are negative. A tie favors the defender.

When an opposed result succeeds or fails by 8 or more, the winning check margin exceeds the losing margin by at least 8. In an unopposed check, failure by 8 or more means a check margin of -8 or lower. Check margin is distinct from an attack's Margin of Success.

Example: Opposed Roll-Under Margins

An attacker with adjusted threshold 20 rolls 23, producing check margin -3. The defender with threshold 18 rolls 20, producing margin -2. Both missed their thresholds, but -2 is the higher and therefore less severe failure, so the defender wins. If both margins had been -2, the defender would also win because opposed ties favor the defender.

Heavy Attack

A Heavy Attack is a special maneuver made with a weapon formally classified as Heavy and wielded in both hands. A non-Heavy two-handed weapon does not qualify.

Heavy Attack has no separate maneuver PE cost. It is performed as a Full Attack and therefore costs the ordinary 1 PE, including when using the one-attack fallback below.

Normally, Heavy Attack consumes two unused attacks from the current round but resolves as one attack during one chosen pass:

  1. Choose a pass while at least two attacks remain unused.
  1. Consume the current attack and one other unused attack from the round.
  1. Make one attack at -2 Attack.

A character with two attacks must therefore Heavy Attack on the first pass. A character with three attacks may attack normally on the first pass and Heavy Attack on the second, consuming the final two attacks.

If the character began the round with only one attack, they may instead make a Heavy Attack at initiative count 0 with -5 Attack instead of -2. This fallback is unavailable when the round began with two or more attacks and the character merely spent or lost attacks until only one remained.

On a hit:

  • Double the number of the weapon's damage dice: 2d6 becomes 4d6, and 1d12 becomes 2d12.
  • Do not double fixed damage modifiers.
  • Increase the attack's effective Margin of Success by 4, then apply that Margin once through the ordinary damage calculation.
  • Add +1 Pen, then resolve Armor Penetration, Armor Grade, material modifiers, and unaffected flat DR under Damage Reduction, Armor Penetration, and Armor Grade.

If the Heavy Attack misses, only the opponent who successfully defended against it may make an attack of opportunity. That response uses the defender's ordinary attack-of-opportunity allowance and procedure.

A 2d6 Heavy Weapon becomes 4d6; if the dice total 15, Strength is +4, and the ordinary Margin is 3, Heavy Attack raises the effective Margin to 7 for 26 raw damage. The weapon's Pen 3 becomes Pen 4. A character who began with only one attack may instead make the Heavy Attack at initiative 0 with -5 Attack. A character who began with two attacks must use Heavy Attack on the first pass.

Grab and Grapple

A Grab is a non-damaging melee attack used to establish physical control. Once control is established, the combatants take two roles:

  • The Controller maintains the hold.
  • The Grappled target is the creature being held. The Controller is not themselves Grappled.

Standing or ground position is descriptive unless an effect such as Takedown applies Prone.

Starting a Grab

Normal Grab is available without special training. Improved Grapple requires an explicit feat, talent, or class feature.

A Grab Attack uses the ordinary armed or unarmed melee Attack formula, followed by the Grab modifiers in this section. It can only be Dodged; it cannot be Parried or Blocked. An unarmed Grab Attack requires at least one free hand. An armed Grab Attack can begin with the weapon used for the Grab in hand, subject to the weapon rules below.

A Grab Attack deals no damage. A specific creature ability may instead deal damage and establish control together.

When a Bash succeeds by 8 or more and grants its free grapple initiation, proceed directly to the post-contact Strength contest. Do not make another Grab Attack and do not grant the defender the special free attack described below.

Weapon Edge and Establishing Control

Weapon Edge applies when the defender has a ready weapon whose reach or threat is clearly superior to the attacker's means of approach. Treat Weapon Edge as one -4 circumstance; do not add separate handedness, reach, or weapon-category modifiers. It stacks with Normal Grab's -2.

Resolve a Grab in this order:

  1. Resolve the defender's free attack. Normal Grab grants one special free attack to an armed defender before the Grab Attack. Improved Grapple prevents this attack unless the defender has Weapon Edge. This is a special free weapon attack resolved normally, not an attack of opportunity, and it does not use the defender's attack-of-opportunity allowance. If it deals at least 1 actual damage, the Grab attempt ends. A hit that results in 0 actual damage does not stop the attempt.
  1. Make the Grab Attack. Resolve the non-damaging attack against Dodge. A smaller target gains +2 Dodge per creature-size category against the Grab Attack.
  1. Set the attacker's weapons. If the Grab Attack connects, the attacker drops every non-Light weapon before the post-contact contest. The attacker may retain one Light weapon.
  1. Contest control. Both participants make opposed roll-under checks using Strength score + BAB. The larger participant gains +8 to their threshold per creature-size category. Compare check margins as described under Roll-Under and Opposed Checks. The higher margin wins, and a tie favors the defender. Dexterity cannot substitute unless an explicit ability permits it.
  1. Assign the roles. If the defender wins, the Grab ends and control does not reverse. If the attacker wins, the attacker becomes the Controller and the defender becomes the Grappled target.

  • Continuing Choke costs one attack in each later round.

  1. If Choke is released, an otherwise unharmed target remains Unconscious for 1d4 rounds, then wakes unless another injury or condition prevents it.
  1. If Choke continues after Unconsciousness, the target's next turn is a complete maintained grace round and causes no LP loss. Character-Scale Combat Units

A Combat Unit represents at least four broadly similar combatants as one character-scale formation. It makes one aggregate attack instead of rolling once per member. Its Effective Strength provides its Melee Formation Bonus, its contributors determine Impact and the Contribution Bonus, and the target defends once. Meeting the unit's Attack removes 1 Impact, and every additional full 4 points of defensive margin removes 1 more. The remaining Impact determines how many sets of weapon damage dice are rolled.

The unit uses one representative profile, one initiative, ordinary Movement, one Main Action, and Attack Speed 1. It cannot Full Attack unless an explicit unit rule permits it. With fewer than four current members, the survivors act individually.

Effective Strength equals current membership, to a maximum of 20.

Actual membership may exceed 20. Surplus members are casualty buffers and replacements: they do not increase that unit's Formation Bonus, available contributors, Impact, or Contribution Bonus. Those values begin declining only when membership falls below 20. A 30-member unit therefore retains Effective Strength 20 until it falls to 19 members. A larger body may instead be divided into several Combat Units, each acting and calculating its values separately.

Recovery and Aftermath

Rest Periods

Vanaheim uses the following rest terms throughout this chapter:

  • Short Rest: A pause that the DM judges sufficient for short-rest recovery in the current circumstances. No universal duration has been established. It permits the HP recovery described below and the deliberately flexible short-rest PE recovery; it does not automatically remove Fatigued or Exhausted.
  • Complete Rest: Rest without strenuous activity for whatever period a rule specifies. Complete Rest describes the quality of the rest, not a separate fixed duration. The one-hour recovery steps for Exhausted and fatigue-induced Unconsciousness use Complete Rest.
  • Long Rest: Eight hours of Complete Rest. Earlier rules may call the same recovery period a Full Rest. A Long Rest restores all naturally restorable HP, restores all PE, and removes the entire fatigue ladder. It does not automatically restore LP.

Reasonable interruptions during a Long Rest are adjudicated by the DM. These rules do not require minute-by-minute tracking or add separate recovery periods to the same eight hours.

Cure Serious Wounds rolls 9 for a living recipient at full HP and 9/12 LP in the Light band. Spend 3 points to restore the recipient to 12/12 LP; the remaining 6 points are unused.

Example: Recovering Through Multiple Wound Bands

A character with maximum LP 15 and Constitution modifier +2 begins at -1 LP under sustained competent care. After seven days they recover to 1 LP and wake. Seven days later they reach 3 LP, and after another seven days they reach 5 LP and enter the Serious band. Their faster Serious rate now applies: three days later they reach 7, and three days after that they reach 9 and enter Light. The Light rate then applies, restoring another 2 LP after one day of care.

Physical Energy and Fatigue Recovery

A Short Rest restores a substantial amount of PE chosen for the circumstances. Common table results are 1d6, 2d6, or full recovery; there is no universal Short-Rest PE formula. Refresh and other abilities restore PE according to their own rules.

A Long Rest restores all PE. The same eight hours of Complete Rest also removes the entire fatigue ladder, so the character does not need one period to clear the condition followed by another period to recover PE.

PE cannot fall below 0. An action that spends the character's final PE finishes before its zero-PE consequence begins. The character then becomes Exhausted, which sets and locks current PE at 0. While Exhausted, the character cannot recover PE through a Short Rest, Refresh, or another ordinary PE-restoration effect. Exhausted must first end or become Fatigued.

Once reduced to Fatigued, the character may recover and spend PE normally. Recovering PE does not itself remove Fatigued, and Refresh does not remove either Fatigued or Exhausted unless an explicit rule says otherwise.

For fatigue-induced conditions, Complete Rest reduces the ladder as follows:

Eight hours of Complete Rest remove any of these fatigue states directly and restore all PE. Do not add the one-hour recovery steps to those eight hours.

Restoring HP or LP does not wake a character made Unconscious by fatigue. Another source of Unconsciousness follows its own recovery rule.

Combat Conditions

General Condition Rules

The effect applying a condition normally determines its duration and removal. When a condition is inherently persistent and no ordinary duration makes sense, it continues until something actually changes that state.

If the applying effect gives no duration or removal procedure, use the condition's own rules.

Receiving the same condition again has no additional effect unless that condition or its source explicitly provides ranks, stacking, escalation, or another reapplication rule.

Fatigued and Exhausted

Fatigue escalates through three applications:

  1. A character who newly receives fatigue becomes Fatigued.
  1. A Fatigued character who receives fatigue becomes Exhausted instead.
  1. An Exhausted character who receives fatigue becomes Unconscious.

Reaching 0 PE bypasses the first step and makes the character Exhausted after the action spending the final PE finishes. An effect that directly applies Exhausted invokes the complete Exhausted condition below.

Fatigued. A Fatigued character:

  • cannot Run or Charge;
  • takes -2 to Attack rolls, Defense rolls, saving throws, and skill checks; and
  • may recover and spend PE normally.

Refresh and ordinary PE recovery do not remove Fatigued. Eight hours of Complete Rest removes it unless the source applying fatigue states otherwise.

Exhausted. Becoming Exhausted sets current PE to 0 and locks it there. An Exhausted character cannot recover PE through Refresh, a Short Rest, or another ordinary PE-restoration effect. The condition must first end or become Fatigued.

Each round, an Exhausted character receives either Movement or one Main Action, never both. Movement is halved. A permitted Main Action may be one ordinary attack, one spell, or another action that costs no PE; it cannot be a Full Attack. Defenses and reactions remain available when they require no PE.

An Exhausted character also:

  • cannot Run or Charge; and
  • takes -6 to Attack rolls, Defense rolls, saving throws, skill checks, and ability checks.

After one hour of Complete Rest, Exhausted becomes Fatigued. A third fatigue application instead causes Unconsciousness, whose complete effects appear below. The recovery procedures for all three stages appear under Physical Energy and Fatigue Recovery.

Prone

While Prone, the selected attack method supplies one Full-Attack pass. An ability that places several attacks inside that pass still does so, but Attack-Speed increases do not add further passes unless an explicit rule says otherwise.

If the character becomes Prone after already resolving an attack in the current round, every unresolved attack is lost. Becoming Prone therefore ends an ongoing Full Attack. A character who has not yet attacked may use the one remaining pass while Prone, subject to the condition's other restrictions.

A Prone character:

  • gains +4 Dodge against ranged attacks; and

Staggered and Dazed

Staggered. Each round, a Staggered character chooses either Movement or one Main Action that is not a Full Attack, never both. The permitted Main Action may be one ordinary attack, one spell, or another single action that does not produce a Full Attack. Haste grants no additional action while the character is Staggered.

Dazed. A Dazed character receives no Movement and no Main Action. Dazed does not itself impose a numeric Defense penalty; any further restriction comes from the effect applying it or another condition.

Unconscious

An Unconscious character falls Prone, drops everything held, and is Helpless. The character receives no Movement or Main Action, cannot speak or take another action, and cannot make active Defenses.

An Unconscious character cannot spend PE, including the 5-PE response that can prevent a Mortal Wound.

Use this static Defense:

10 + applicable passive magical defensive modifiers

Do not add BAB, Dexterity, weapon or shield bonuses, or another active-defense benefit. A deliberate attack that genuinely connects with the helpless body may deal Lethal Damage as described under Damage and Protection.

Stunned and Paralyzed

Stunned. A Stunned character drops everything held, receives no Movement or Main Action, and cannot voluntarily leave their space. Another creature or external force may still move the character.

The character may Parry, Block, Dodge, make otherwise eligible defensive reactions, and spend PE. Remove the character's Dexterity modifier from every Defense roll. PE cannot suppress this loss of Dexterity, although the character may use the ordinary 1-PE option to suppress one other eligible negative modifier on that roll.

Defensive movement cannot carry the Stunned character out of their space. Every successful hit against the character is a critical hit. Resolve that critical through HP normally unless the attack independently qualifies as Lethal Damage.

Paralyzed. A Paralyzed character cannot move, speak, take physical actions, or make active Defenses. The character is Helpless. A purely mental action remains possible only when its own rules require no speech, movement, somatic component, or other physical act.

Against an attack, use static Defense:

10 + applicable passive magical defensive modifiers

Do not add BAB, Dexterity, weapon or shield bonuses, or another active-defense benefit.

A winged creature that becomes Paralyzed while flying falls. A swimming creature cannot swim and may drown according to the circumstances.

A deliberate attack that genuinely connects with the helpless body may deal Lethal Damage and bypass HP. Paralysis does not separately make every hit an automatic critical hit.

Fear has three cumulative stages: Shaken, Frightened, and Panicked. An effect may apply a stated stage directly. Further fear escalates an affected character as follows:

  • Shaken applied to a Shaken character becomes Frightened.

  • Frightened applied to a Shaken character becomes Panicked.
  • Shaken or Frightened applied to a Frightened character becomes Panicked.
  • Panicked is the highest general fear stage.

Fear penalties do not stack; use only the total penalty imposed by the character's current fear stage.

Shaken. A Shaken character takes -2 to Attack rolls, saving throws, skill checks, and ability checks. This includes Fortitude saves. Shaken does not itself impose a Defense penalty.

Frightened. A Frightened character takes -4 to Attack rolls, saving throws, skill checks, and ability checks. This is the total Frightened penalty, not an additional penalty stacked on Shaken.

Each round, the character chooses either Movement or one Main Action that is not a Full Attack, never both. Defenses and otherwise eligible reactions remain available.

If within the fear source's melee reach, the character must leave that reach if able and cannot voluntarily move closer to the source. The character cannot deliberately harm the fear source. They may ordinarily attack other enemies, heal, assist, defend, or take another supportive action. The DM may broaden this restriction when the nature or circumstances of the fear make other hostile action implausible.

Every spell a Frightened character attempts has a flat 25% failure chance, including arcane, divine, offensive, defensive, and supportive spells. A spell lost to this failure still spends its full ME cost.

Panicked. A Panicked character takes a total -4 penalty to Attack rolls, saving throws, skill checks, and ability checks. This replaces rather than stacks with the Shaken penalty. The character drops everything held and uses their actions to flee the fear source at maximum speed. The character flees other immediate dangers rather than confronting them. If prevented from fleeing, the character cowers and cannot attack. Ordinary movement hazards and attacks of opportunity still apply while fleeing.

Power Scale and Domain Ranks

Domain Ranks describe the scale at which a specific attribute, attack, defense, spell, or ability operates. A rank belongs only to the value or power carrying it. It does not raise every

statistic of the creature unless the creature's rules explicitly assign that rank more broadly.

An ordinary statistic without an explicit Domain Rank operates at D rank.

Rank Bands

RankScale
DMortal or ordinary scale. Normal statistics and ordinary d20 mathematics control.
CExceptional, supernatural, or heroic scale. It dominates ordinary opposition but remains contestable by comparable specialists, preparation, tools, or circumstances.
BLegendary or monstrous scale. Direct contests against lower ranks usually require exceptional leverage.
AGodlike scale. Its power belongs to gods and comparable beings.
AAWorld-altering, avatar, true-ancestor, or conceptual scale. It can change battlefields, nations, natural laws, or metaphysical conditions.
SSpecial and incomparable. S sits outside the ordinary ladder; every S-ranked power defines what it can and cannot do.

Applying Rank Gaps

Identify the rank governing the action and the rank governing its opposition. Compare those ranks even when they come from different attributes or abilities. A Strength-based Attack opposed by a Dexterity-based Dodge therefore compares the attacker's Strength rank with the defender's Dexterity rank. Combatants will normally favor their highest-ranked legal form of offense or defense.

Rank gapCombat result
Same rankApply no rank modifier. Use ordinary statistics, level, skill, equipment, circumstances, and dice.
Higher by one rankThe higher-ranked side gains +10 in every independently resolved formula governed by that domain during the exchange. The lower-ranked side can still compete.
Higher by two ranksThe higher-ranked side gains +40 in every independently resolved formula governed by that domain during the exchange. A direct victory by the lower-ranked side normally requires leverage, a weakness, a relic, a counter-technique, or comparable preparation.
Higher by three or more ranksThere is no meaningful direct contest. The lower-ranked side needs a force multiplier or must change the nature of the contest.
S involvedApply the S-ranked power's explicit rule instead of deriving a numeric result from rank distance.

Apply the rank modifier directly to the relevant formula; never add it to the underlying attribute score or use it to recalculate the ordinary ability modifier.

  • A higher-ranked offense adds the rank modifier to its Attack. If it hits, it also adds the same modifier directly to damage. These are separate applications: the Attack bonus can increase Margin of Success, and the damage calculation still receives the direct rank bonus.
  • A higher-ranked defense adds the rank modifier to its Defense. The Attack and Defense modifiers are determined by the ranks actually governing those two sides of the exchange.
  • The higher-ranked side in a roll-under check adds the modifier once to its threshold. The resulting check margin inherits that increase; do not add the rank modifier to the check margin again.
  • Any other independently resolved check, save, DC, or value receives the modifier when the ranked domain directly governs it. If that modified value is then used to calculate another result, do not apply the rank modifier again to the new result. Do not add Strength rank to Initiative, Will, spellcasting, or another unrelated domain.

A lower-ranked combatant may still prevail by attacking a different domain, exploiting terrain or information, using allies or a suitable relic, invoking a specific counter, or otherwise avoiding the direct comparison.

Conditional and Special Ranks

A + or - after a rank marks a conditional shift, not an ordinary numeric modifier. The ability must state the condition that causes the shift.

  • B+ operates at B rank normally and becomes A rank when its stated condition is met.
  • C++ operates at C rank normally and can rise two steps to A rank when its stated condition is met.
  • A+ operates at A rank normally and becomes AA rank when its stated condition is met.
  • B++ operates at B rank normally and can rise two steps to AA rank when its stated condition is met.
  • C- operates at C rank normally and falls to D rank under its stated condition.

If the condition is not active, use the printed letter without its signs. Conditional shifts remain within D through AA and cannot enter S. S-ranked abilities always state their own operation, limits, and interactions.

Example: Strength 18B Against C-Ranked Opposition

A dragon has Strength 18B. Its opponent has Strength 24C. The opponent has the higher raw Strength score, but the dragon's Strength operates one rank higher.

For a direct opposed Strength check, the dragon receives the one-rank +10. In a pure roll-under contest, its adjusted threshold is 18 + 10 = 28, while the opponent's threshold remains 24. Any BAB, size, equipment, or situational modifiers required by the particular maneuver are then applied normally, and both sides compare check margins.

If the dragon rolls 12, its check margin is 28 - 12 = 16. If the opponent rolls 10, their check margin is 24 - 10 = 14; the dragon wins by 2. The C-ranked opponent can still win with a sufficiently better roll because a one-rank gap remains contestable.

For a weapon attack, compare the dragon's B-ranked Strength offense with the rank governing the opponent's chosen Defense. If the opponent Dodges with C-ranked Dexterity, the dragon adds +10 to its Strength-based Attack and, if the attack connects, adds +10 directly to damage. Suppose the dragon's ordinary Attack total is 22 and the opponent's Defense is 20. Rank raises the Attack to 32, producing Margin 12. The raw damage calculation then uses the weapon dice, the dragon's unchanged ordinary Strength modifier, Margin 12, and the direct +10 rank bonus. Protection resolves normally afterward.

The +10 Attack contribution has already flowed into Margin and is not added to Margin again. The separate +10 damage contribution is nevertheless applied because Strength governs the damage formula as well. If the opponent instead used a B-ranked Defense, the ranks would be equal and neither rank modifier would apply.

The notation never turns the dragon's Strength score into 28, changes its attribute maximum, or produces a new Strength modifier. Strength remains 18 for every ordinary calculation; +10 is the independent effect of the one-rank gap. Against D-ranked opposition, a B-ranked Strength offense receives +40 in each independently resolved relevant formula instead.
